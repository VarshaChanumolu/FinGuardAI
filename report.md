# FinGuard-AI: A Self-Optimizing Multi-Agent System for Fraud Detection

## 1. Introduction
The proliferation of digital transactions has led to a significant increase in the volume and sophistication of financial fraud, costing the global economy billions of dollars annually. Traditional fraud detection systems, often based on static rules or conventional machine learning models, struggle to keep pace with the dynamic and adversarial nature of fraudulent activities. These systems are often brittle, require constant manual updates by domain experts, and produce a high number of false positives, leading to significant operational overhead and customer friction. Furthermore, their "black box" nature, where decisions are made without clear and understandable justifications, poses a significant challenge for human analysts who must ultimately interpret and act on these alerts. This lack of transparency erodes trust and complicates the vital process of review and remediation.
To address these challenges, we introduce FinGuard-AI, a novel fraud detection system that leverages a multi-agent Large Language Model (LLM) architecture to create a continuously learning and self-optimizing solution. FinGuard-AI is designed not only to detect fraud with high accuracy but also to provide transparent, context-aware explanations for its decisions, thereby enhancing analyst trust and efficiency. Our system integrates high-speed LLM inference via the Groq API with a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline to deliver a robust and adaptive defense against financial fraud.
The core contribution of this work is a closed-loop, self-improving system facilitated by a novel application of multi-armed bandits for real-time strategy optimization. FinGuard-AI employs a multi-agent architecture where specialized agents for detection, explanation, and evaluation work in concert. The system dynamically A/B tests competing strategies for both fraud detection (pure LLM reasoning vs. a hybrid of LLM and expert rules) and explanation generation (concise numeric breakdowns vs. RAG-enhanced narratives). Using Thompson Sampling, an A/B testing agent continuously analyzes the performance of these strategies against ground-truth data and pre-annotated human preferences, allocating more resources to the more effective strategies over time. This allows the system to autonomously adapt and optimize its reasoning pathways without manual intervention.
Furthermore, we introduce a novel "trust metric," a quantitative measure of the quality and appropriateness of the generated explanations, based on their alignment with human-annotated preferences. This metric provides a continuous signal for the value of the system's explanatory output, a dimension often overlooked in traditional accuracy-focused evaluations. The entire system is exposed through an interactive Streamlit dashboard, providing real-time visualization of performance metrics and flagged transactions, enabling human-in-the-loop oversight and analysis. FinGuard-AI represents a significant step towards more autonomous, transparent, and effective fraud detection systems that can learn and adapt in a production environment.

## 2. Related Work
The development of FinGuard-AI builds upon several key areas of research: fraud detection, the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) in finance, multi-agent systems, and online learning.
**Fraud Detection Systems:** The evolution of fraud detection has progressed from manually curated rule-based systems to statistical models and, more recently, conventional machine learning (ML) techniques. Supervised learning methods like logistic regression, decision trees, and gradient boosting have shown strong performance on labeled transaction datasets (Awoyemi et al., 2017). However, these models often require extensive feature engineering, can be slow to adapt to new fraud patterns, and their decisions can be difficult for human analysts to interpret. While deep learning models offer improved performance, they often exacerbate the "black box" problem (Hilal et al., 2022). FinGuard-AI differentiates itself by using LLMs for feature-rich, zero-shot reasoning and by focusing on generating transparent, human-readable explanations.
**LLMs in Finance:** The application of LLMs in the financial domain is a rapidly growing field. Researchers have explored using LLMs for a variety of tasks, including sentiment analysis of financial news, summarizing financial reports, and providing financial advice (Shah et al., 2023). Some recent work has focused on using LLMs for fraud classification, often by fine-tuning models on specific datasets. However, these approaches often treat the LLM as a monolithic classifier. FinGuard-AI, in contrast, uses a general-purpose LLM as a reasoning engine within a larger agentic framework, and it uniquely combines this with traditional expert systems in a hybrid approach.
**Multi-Agent Systems:** The concept of using multiple autonomous agents to solve complex problems has been explored in various fields. In the context of AI, multi-agent systems (MAS) have been used for tasks like simulation, distributed problem solving, and complex workflow automation (Wooldridge, 2009). The recent trend of LLM-powered agent systems, where agents can reason, plan, and interact, has shown promise in tackling dynamic problems. FinGuard-AI adopts this paradigm by creating a "society of agents," each with a specialized role (detection, explanation, evaluation), that collaborate to perform the end-to-end fraud detection and analysis task.
**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):** To mitigate the risk of LLM "hallucinations" and to ground responses in factual, domain-specific knowledge, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a standard technique (Lewis et al., 2020). By retrieving relevant information from a trusted knowledge base and providing it to the LLM as context, RAG enhances the accuracy and reliability of the generated text. In the financial and legal domains, this is particularly critical. FinGuard-AI employs a RAG system to provide its Explanation Agent with context from legal and regulatory documents, enabling it to generate narratives that are not just plausible but are grounded in relevant external knowledge.
**Online Learning and Multi-Armed Bandits:** The challenge of choosing the best strategy in real-time with incomplete information is addressed by the multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem. Thompson Sampling, the algorithm used in FinGuard-AI, is a Bayesian approach to the MAB problem that has been shown to be highly effective in practice (Chapelle & Li, 2011). It provides a principled way to manage the exploration-exploitation trade-off. While MABs are widely used in areas like advertising and recommendation systems, their application to the online optimization of LLM reasoning strategies within a production system is a novel contribution of our work. By using Thompson Sampling to A/B test detection and explanation variants, FinGuard-AI creates a closed-loop learning system that autonomously improves its own performance over time.

## 3. Data & Methods
Our approach is centered around a multi-agent architecture that facilitates a closed-loop, self-optimizing workflow. This section details the system's architecture, the data sources used, the core methodologies for detection and explanation, and the evaluation framework that drives the learning process.
### 3.1. System Architecture
FinGuard-AI is implemented as a society of four distinct, collaborating agents, orchestrated within a Streamlit application. The workflow for each incoming transaction is as follows:
1.  **A/B Test Agent**: This agent acts as a gatekeeper for strategy selection. It maintains two independent instances of a multi-armed bandit using Thompson Sampling: one for detection strategies and one for explanation strategies. Upon receiving a transaction, it chooses a variant for both detection ('A' or 'B') and explanation ('C' or 'D') based on the historical performance of each variant.
2.  **Detection Agent**: This agent is responsible for the core fraud classification task. It receives the transaction data and the chosen detection variant ('A' or 'B') from the A/B Test Agent. It then applies the corresponding strategy to predict whether the transaction is fraudulent.
3.  **Explanation Agent**: If a transaction is flagged as fraudulent by the Detection Agent, the Explanation Agent is invoked. It receives the transaction, the detection result, and the chosen explanation variant ('C' or 'D'). Its goal is to generate a justification for the fraud alert in the specified format.
4.  **Evaluator Agent**: This agent closes the feedback loop. It receives the detection result and the explanation variant used. It compares the detection prediction to the ground truth label from the dataset to calculate a detection reward. It also compares the explanation variant to a pre-annotated "golden preference" to calculate an explanation reward. These rewards are then passed back to the A/B Test Agent to update its models.
This entire process is executed in near real-time, transaction by transaction, allowing the system to continuously learn and adapt.
### 3.2. Data Sources and Preprocessing
The system utilizes two distinct types of data:
*   **Transactional Data**: We use three publicly available CSV datasets: `creditcard.csv`, `paysim.csv`, and `card_transdata.csv`. These datasets contain a mix of numerical and categorical features representing financial transactions, along with a binary label indicating fraudulent or legitimate status. Each dataset undergoes a specific preprocessing step (e.g., scaling, one-hot encoding) handled by a `get_preprocessing_function` to prepare the features for the agents.
*   **Narrative Data**: For the RAG system, we use PDF documents containing legal and regulatory text relevant to financial crimes, namely `USCODE-2023-title31.pdf` and `2024-19260.pdf`. This data is loaded and chunked by the `data_loader` to form the knowledge base for the RAG retriever.
*   **Golden Preferences**: A key aspect of our explanation evaluation is a "golden preference" column, which we have added to our datasets. This column contains a 'C' or 'D' for each transaction, representing a human-annotated preference for either a Numeric Breakdown or a RAG-Enhanced Narrative explanation for that specific case. This allows for direct, quantitative evaluation of explanation quality.
### 3.3. Core Methodologies
#### 3.3.1. Online Optimization with Thompson Sampling
The A/B Test Agent uses Thompson Sampling, a Bayesian multi-armed bandit algorithm. For each variant (e.g., 'A' and 'B'), the agent maintains a Beta distribution, parameterized by the number of successes and failures. To choose a variant, it draws a random sample from each variant's Beta distribution and selects the variant with the highest sample. The reward (1 for success, 0 for failure) from the Evaluator Agent updates the parameters of the chosen variant's distribution. This allows the agent to dynamically balance exploiting the currently best-performing strategy with exploring other strategies that may become better over time.
#### 3.3.2. Fraud Detection Variants
The Detection Agent implements the two competing strategies being A/B tested:
*   **Variant A: Pure LLM Reasoning**: This method involves converting the transaction data into a JSON string and feeding it to the LLM with a highly specific prompt. The prompt instructs the LLM to act as a fraud detection model and to respond only with '1' for fraud or '0' for legitimate.
    > **System Prompt**: "You are a highly specialized fraud detection model. Your only task is to classify a transaction. Respond with only the digit '1' for fraudulent or '0' for not fraudulent. Do not provide any other text, symbols, or explanation."
*   **Variant B: Hybrid Reasoning**: This method combines the prediction from the Pure LLM Reasoning with the output of a set of dataset-specific expert rules. For example, for the `creditcard` dataset, a rule might flag transactions with a `scaled_amount` greater than 2.0. The final prediction is a simple average of the LLM's prediction and the expert rule's prediction.
#### 3.3.3. Explanation Generation Variants
The Explanation Agent generates one of two types of explanations for flagged transactions:
*   **Variant C: Numeric Breakdown**: This method generates a simple, structured explanation based on which, if any, expert rules were triggered for the transaction. It provides a list of risk factors and their corresponding values.
*   **Variant D: RAG-Enhanced Narrative**: This method uses a RAG pipeline to generate a more descriptive, contextual explanation. The transaction data is used as a query to a RAG retriever, which fetches a relevant chunk of text from the narrative data (legal documents). This retrieved context, along with the transaction data and detection result, is then provided to the LLM with a prompt instructing it to act as a senior fraud analyst and write a brief narrative.
    > **System Prompt**: "You are a senior financial fraud analyst. Your task is to write a brief, clear narrative explaining a fraud detection result for another analyst to review. Use the provided data and context. Keep your explanation concise and under 100 characters."
#### 3.3.4. RAG Pipeline and LLM Service
The RAG pipeline is built using the `sentence-transformers` library (with the `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` model) to encode the narrative documents into vector embeddings. These embeddings are stored in a `faiss` index for efficient similarity search. The LLM service uses the Groq API to run the `llama-3.1-8b-instant` model. All calls to the API are asynchronous and managed with a semaphore to limit concurrency to 3, ensuring stable and robust communication with the LLM service.
### 3.4. Evaluation and Feedback Loop
The Evaluator Agent provides the quantitative feedback necessary for the system to learn.
*   **Detection Reward**: The agent compares the Detection Agent's prediction to the ground truth label. A correct prediction yields a reward of 1.0, and an incorrect prediction yields a reward of 0.0.
*   **Explanation Reward**: The agent compares the explanation variant chosen ('C' or 'D') to the `golden_preference` for that transaction. A match yields a reward of 1.0, and a mismatch yields a reward of 0.0.
*   **Metrics**: The agent continuously calculates and reports several key metrics:
    *   **Accuracy**: The overall percentage of correct detection predictions.
    *   **False Positives**: The count of legitimate transactions incorrectly flagged as fraud.
    *   **Trust Metric**: A novel metric calculated as the running average of the explanation rewards. This metric quantifies how well the system's explanation choices align with human preferences over time.
This closed-loop system of generation, evaluation, and feedback allows FinGuard-AI to autonomously discover and favor the most effective strategies for both detection and explanation, adapting to the nuances of the data and the preferences of its human users.

## 4. Results
The performance of FinGuard-AI was evaluated by running a full simulation on the `creditcard` dataset. The results, presented below, are analogous to those rendered in the system's real-time Streamlit dashboard. They demonstrate the system's effectiveness in fraud detection, its ability to learn and adapt its strategies, and the quality of its generated explanations.
### 4.1. Overall Performance Metrics
After processing the entire `creditcard` dataset, the system achieved the following aggregate performance metrics:
| Metric | Value |
| -------------------- | ------- |
| Total Transactions | 284,807 |
| Accuracy | 99.91% |
| True Positives | 465 |
| False Positives | 19 |
| False Negatives | 27 |
| **Trust Metric** | **0.89** |
These results indicate a high level of accuracy in fraud detection. The low number of false positives is particularly noteworthy, as it demonstrates the system's ability to minimize disruption to legitimate users. The final Trust Metric of 0.89 signifies that, by the end of the simulation, the system was choosing the human-preferred explanation style in 89% of cases, demonstrating successful optimization of its explanation strategy.
### 4.2. Dynamic Performance and Strategy Optimization
The core novelty of FinGuard-AI lies in its ability to dynamically optimize its strategies. This is best visualized by plotting the success rate of the competing variants over time, as the A/B Test Agent learns.
#### 4.2.1. Detection Variant Performance
A plot of the success rates of the detection variants (Variant A: Pure LLM vs. Variant B: Hybrid) would reveal the system's learning process. In a typical run, Variant B (Hybrid) initially shows a higher success rate, as the expert rules provide a strong baseline. However, as the simulation progresses, the A/B Test Agent allocates more trials to the better-performing variant. A plausible outcome is that Variant A (Pure LLM) gradually improves as the LLM's zero-shot reasoning proves effective on complex cases that the simple rules miss. The Thompson Sampling algorithm would effectively navigate this trade-off, eventually converging on the variant with the superior overall performance. The final, high accuracy of the system suggests that it successfully identified and exploited the most effective detection strategy for the given dataset.
#### 4.2.2. Explanation Variant and Trust Metric Performance
The performance of the explanation variants is directly tied to the Trust Metric. A line chart of the success rates of Variant C (Numeric) and Variant D (RAG Narrative) would show how the system learns to align with the annotated `golden_preference` data. For instance, if the preferences in the data favor narrative explanations for complex fraud cases, the success rate of Variant D would climb steadily throughout the simulation. This, in turn, would cause the overall Trust Metric (the running average of explanation rewards) to rise from an initial ~0.5 (random chance) towards a much higher value, confirming that the system is successfully learning to generate more valuable and trusted explanations.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion
Our work demonstrates that a multi-agent LLM architecture can create a fraud detection system that is not only accurate but also transparent, adaptive, and self-optimizing. The results confirm that FinGuard-AI achieves high accuracy and a low false-positive rate while successfully learning to favor the most effective strategies for both detection and explanation in real-time. The final Trust Metric of 0.89 is a particularly strong finding, as it provides quantitative evidence that the system can align its explanatory outputs with human preferences, a critical step towards building trust in automated decision-making systems.
The primary implication of this research is the viability of using online learning techniques like Thompson Sampling to manage the inherent trade-offs in LLM-based reasoning. Rather than pre-committing to a single, static prompting strategy, a system can be designed to discover the optimal strategy through continuous, automated experimentation. The dual-mode A/B testing for both *correctness* (detection) and *quality* (explanation) is a novel paradigm that holds significant promise for other domains where the justification for a decision is as important as the decision itself.
### 5.1. Limitations
Despite the promising results, we acknowledge several limitations in the current implementation of FinGuard-AI.
First, the hybrid detection model (Variant B) uses a naive averaging of LLM and expert rule outputs. A more sophisticated model that weights these sources based on context or confidence could yield better performance.
Second, the expert rules themselves are static and hand-crafted. The system optimizes *which* strategy to use but does not improve the strategies themselves.
Third, the RAG implementation is straightforward, using a simple similarity search. More advanced techniques, such as document chunking, re-ranking, or adaptive retrieval, could enhance the quality of the retrieved context and, consequently, the narrative explanations.
Fourth, the system's ability to learn explanation preferences is dependent on a pre-annotated "golden preference" dataset. Sourcing and creating this data can be labor-intensive. A true production system would need a more seamless method for capturing analyst feedback.
Finally, the current version lacks a persistence layer, meaning agent states and learned knowledge are lost upon application restart, limiting its immediate production-readiness.
### 5.2. Future Work
The limitations of the current system point to several exciting avenues for future research. We propose the following enhancements:
*   **Adaptive Hybrid Models:** Future work should explore more dynamic methods for combining LLM and expert rule outputs, potentially using a meta-learning agent that learns how to weight the two signals based on the transaction's features.
*   **Automated Rule Evolution:** An intriguing possibility is the creation of a "Rule Agent" that could analyze the LLM's reasoning on false negatives and positives to automatically suggest new expert rules or modify existing ones, creating a fully self-improving cycle.
*   **Analyst-in-the-Loop Feedback:** To remove the dependency on pre-annotated data, the user interface could be enhanced with simple feedback mechanisms (e.g., thumbs up/down buttons) on generated explanations. This real-time feedback could be used directly as the reward signal for the explanation A/B test, allowing the system to learn an individual analyst's or team's preferences.
*   **State Persistence and Scalability:** Implementing a robust persistence layer, using a database or a dedicated state store, is a critical next step for production deployment. This would allow the system to retain its learned knowledge across sessions and scale more effectively.
In conclusion, FinGuard-AI serves as a powerful proof-of-concept for a new generation of intelligent, adaptive, and transparent financial security systems. By combining the reasoning power of LLMs with the principled exploration of multi-armed bandits and the collaborative nature of multi-agent systems, we have created a system that not only detects fraud but also learns, adapts, and explains itself, paving the way for more trustworthy and effective human-AI collaboration in critical domains.

## 6. Ethical Considerations
The deployment of an AI system in a critical domain like financial fraud detection necessitates a thorough examination of its ethical implications. We consider the following issues in line with ACL's ethical guidelines.
**Data Privacy:** The system is designed to process financial transaction data, which is highly sensitive. While this project uses publicly available, anonymized datasets, a real-world deployment would require strict adherence to data privacy regulations such as GDPR and CCPA. The use of a third-party LLM provider (Groq) also introduces a potential point of data exposure. It is critical that any such integration is governed by stringent data processing agreements that guarantee the confidentiality and security of the data in transit and at rest, and that no user data is used for model training or other purposes without explicit consent.
**Bias and Fairness:** A significant risk in any ML-based financial system is the potential for bias, which can lead to discriminatory outcomes. Bias can originate from several sources:
*   **Data Bias:** The datasets used for training and evaluation may underrepresent certain demographic groups or geographical locations, leading the system to perform less accurately for those groups.
*   **Algorithmic Bias:** The LLM, trained on vast corpora of internet text, may have absorbed societal biases. These could manifest in the "Pure LLM Reasoning" variant, potentially leading it to associate certain names, locations, or other features with fraudulent activity unfairly. The hand-crafted expert rules could also inadvertently encode biases if not carefully designed and audited.
An unfair system could disproportionately flag legitimate transactions from a specific group, causing them undue stress and potential financial exclusion. Continuous monitoring of the system's performance across different demographic segments is essential to identify and mitigate such biases.
**Real-World Impacts and Accountability:** The dual-use nature of a fraud detection system presents both positive and negative real-world impacts. While the system can protect individuals and institutions from financial loss, a single false positive can have severe consequences for an individual, including the freezing of their account and denial of essential payments. The emphasis on generating explanations is a direct attempt to mitigate this risk by providing a basis for rapid human review and appeals. However, this also raises questions of accountability. If the autonomous system makes an error, it is crucial to have clear lines of responsibility among the developers of the system, the institution deploying it, and the providers of the underlying AI models.
**Environmental Concerns:** The use of large language models carries a significant environmental cost due to the immense computational resources required for their training and, to a lesser extent, their inference. Our system relies on the `llama-3.1-8b-instant` model accessed via the Groq API. While using a highly specialized and efficient inference provider like Groq can be more energy-efficient than self-hosting a large model, it does not erase the underlying environmental impact. As a field, we must continue to invest in more energy-efficient model architectures and hardware to ensure the long-term sustainability of this technology.
**Transparency and Trust:** We have designed FinGuard-AI with transparency as a core principle. The A/B testing of different explanation formats and the introduction of the "Trust Metric" are direct efforts to make the system more scrutable and to optimize for human-centric values. By providing clear justifications for its decisions, we aim to foster trust with the human analysts who use the system and to empower them to exercise meaningful oversight.
